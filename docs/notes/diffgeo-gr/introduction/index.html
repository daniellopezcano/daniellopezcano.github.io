1 Introduction

Chapter 1

Introduction

I believe that the best way to learn any topic in physics is to first build a solid understanding of its mathematical foundations. Intuition matters too, but the further a physical process lies from our everyday experience, the less obvious it becomes what that intuition should look like. This is especially true for quantum mechanics and general relativity, where intuition only develops after working through (and practising with) the underlying mathematics.

The same holds for topics you think you already understand: revisiting them with a firmer grasp of their mathematics, you rediscover them with new depth and a richer intuition. And because different theories often share the same mathematical framework, insight into how a mechanism works in one well-known theory can often be carried over to another.

Differential geometry is one such framework. It provides a general and robust foundation for many of today’s fundamental theories in physics. It is not uncommon to find colleagues in astrophysics and cosmology who regard differential geometry merely as a “necessary evil” for learning general relativity. I cannot emphasise enough how central it is to physics as a whole: even if you can get away without it for many “basic” calculations in classical mechanics, electromagnetism, or special relativity, it is still the backbone holding these theories together. Hamiltonian mechanics lives on a symplectic manifold (phase space), Maxwell’s equations reduce to two compact equations for the electromagnetic field-strength 2-form, and special relativity is nothing but the geometry of Minkowski spacetime.

Still, most undergraduate physics programmes avoid the subject, at least partially, relying instead on hand-waving arguments. In practice, crowded curricula and limited preparation time often mean that students graduate with only a superficial exposure to the topic. This brief and incomplete contact can leave the impression that differential geometry is an arcane subject best left untouched, when in reality it is an essential tool that is far more approachable than its reputation suggests.

Differential geometry is a beautiful subject that can be understood with a bit of effort and patience. Don’t be put off by big words like “manifold”, “tensor”, or “connection”: they are simply precise mathematical names for intuitions you already have about the physical world, perhaps without realising it.

In these lecture notes, I aim to give a compact, pedagogical introduction to the subject that equips the reader with the essential tools needed to understand the mathematical foundations of general relativity. The notes assume familiarity with linear algebra and multivariable calculus; some prior exposure to special relativity is helpful but not strictly necessary.

These notes began as a personal project: I wanted to learn general relativity at a more fundamental level, acquiring along the way the mathematical tools needed to appreciate its subtleties. My starting point was the lectures by Prof. Frederic P. Schuller at the WE-Heraeus International Winter School on Gravity and Light (Schuller, 2015).1 These lectures are a fantastic introduction to the subject, and I highly recommend them to anyone interested. Schuller is a great teacher: his lectures are pedagogical and, at times, genuinely funny.

While taking notes on these lectures, I started developing my own Python package to illustrate some of the concepts visually.2 I also began adding explicit calculations and examples to make the key ideas more transparent. Compared with the original lectures, these notes reorganise the material into what I find a more linear sequence of topics, add worked examples and detailed proofs in the appendices, and include computational illustrations throughout. The result is a set of lecture notes I thought were worth sharing.

Before diving in, let me give you the overall picture that motivates our goals. Schuller opens his lectures with a one-sentence summary of general relativity that I find a perfect starting point:

“ Spacetime is a four-dimensional topological manifold with a smooth orientable atlas carrying a torsion-free connection compatible with a Lorentzian metric and a time orientation satisfying the Einstein equations. ”

Of course, that sentence is packed with jargon. But, as with most things in physics, the concepts become transparent once you break them down into smaller, more digestible parts. That is exactly what the following chapters do, each one unpacking a piece of this sentence:

  • Chapter 2 lays the algebraic groundwork. We start with sets, maps, and algebraic structures, then move on to vector spaces, dual spaces, and tensors. These concepts may look abstract, but they are exactly what physics needs: tensors are the objects that allow us to write physical laws without committing to any particular basis (and, later, to any particular coordinate system).

  • Chapter 3 formalises the idea of a space that looks locally like \(\mathbb{R}^n\) but can have a nontrivial global structure: the topological manifold of our opening sentence, which becomes our mathematical model for spacetime. Charts provide local coordinates, and equipping the manifold with a smooth atlas (a collection of charts whose overlaps are smoothly compatible) is what allows us to do calculus on it.

  • Chapter 4 builds, at each point of the manifold, the stage on which vectors and covectors live. A tangent vector is defined as the velocity of a curve through the point, acting on functions as a directional derivative. The tangent vectors at a point form a vector space, the tangent space, whose dual is the cotangent space: the home of covectors such as gradients. Combining both through tensor products yields the tensor spaces at that point. Every chart induces a basis in each of these spaces, and the way components change from one chart to another, which is what distinguishes covariant from contravariant objects, is derived rather than postulated. In this sense, the chapter is the algebra of Chapter 2 applied point by point.

  • Chapter 5 lets these objects vary from point to point. Gluing all the tangent (or cotangent) spaces together produces a new manifold, the tangent (or cotangent) bundle, and a vector, covector, or tensor field is then a smooth assignment of one such object to every point, i.e., a smooth section of the corresponding bundle. The numbers that rescale fields are now replaced by functions on the manifold, so fields form a module rather than a vector space, which explains why, in general, a basis of fields exists only locally. Fields are essential in physics, and here they are introduced without reference to any coordinate system; components appear only afterwards, as functions defined on a chart domain. This clears up a common source of confusion: treating a field and its components as the same thing. The chapter closes by fixing the compact index notation used throughout the rest of the notes.

  • Chapter 6 addresses the problem of differentiation on manifolds. Ordinary derivatives fail because vectors at different points live in different tangent spaces, and there is no built-in way to compare them. A connection provides exactly such a prescription: the resulting covariant derivative generalises the ordinary derivative to tensors and gives a precise meaning to “straight lines” (autoparallels). Every connection comes with two characteristic tensors, its torsion and its curvature; the torsion-free condition in our opening sentence concerns the former, while the latter is the mathematical heart of gravity. A physically illuminating by-product is that pseudo-forces, such as the centrifugal and Coriolis forces, appear as connection coefficients when we change coordinates, revealing them as artefacts of the coordinate choice. In flat space they can be removed everywhere by a suitable change of coordinates; in curved space, only at a single point, a fact that will return as the equivalence principle.

  • Chapter 7 introduces the metric tensor, the object that measures distances, angles, and durations. In general relativity the metric is the gravitational field itself, so understanding its properties and the structures it induces (light cones, proper time, geodesics) is essential for linking geometry to physical predictions. This is where the Lorentzian metric and the time orientation of our opening sentence enter. Metric and connection are, a priori, independent structures. They become linked only when we require the connection to be torsion-free and compatible with the metric, which singles out a unique connection (the Levi-Civita connection) whose straightest curves are also curves of extremal length. The metric also provides our first action principle: the length (or proper-time) functional, whose stationary curves are precisely the geodesics.

  • Chapter 8 develops the machinery to “sum” quantities over a manifold in a coordinate-independent way. Volume forms, which require the manifold to be orientable, make integration well defined, and the general Stokes theorem unifies the fundamental theorem of calculus, Gauss’s divergence theorem, and the classical Stokes theorem of vector calculus, extending them to curved spacetime.

  • Chapter in preparation explores the role of symmetry in geometry and physics. In general relativity, symmetries of the spacetime metric, generated by Killing vector fields, correspond to conserved quantities for particles and fields moving in that spacetime. This deep connection between geometry and conservation laws, in the spirit of Noether’s theorem, is a recurring theme in modern theoretical physics.

  • Chapter 9 revisits Newtonian gravity from a geometric viewpoint, showing that even “classical” gravity can be understood as curvature: the curvature lives in the connection of spacetime, while space itself remains flat. This chapter bridges familiar territory and the more abstract concepts of general relativity.

  • Chapter 10 brings everything together to present general relativity as a physical theory, completing our opening sentence: spacetime geometry governed by the Einstein equations. All the mathematical tools developed in the previous chapters now find their natural role in describing and predicting physical phenomena.

Throughout these notes, we will keep returning to one key idea: coordinates are just labels, a convenient bookkeeping device with no physical substance of their own. We will see it at work in the construction of fields in Chapter 3 and in the pseudo-forces of Chapter 6, and we will make it explicit at every step, peeling away the illusion that the physics depends on a particular set of numbers. The real content lives in the geometry itself, in structures and relationships that remain untouched no matter how we choose to describe them. The rest is about building up this framework piece by piece, connecting the mathematics to the physics as we go. So, enough warm-up: let’s get our hands dirty.
