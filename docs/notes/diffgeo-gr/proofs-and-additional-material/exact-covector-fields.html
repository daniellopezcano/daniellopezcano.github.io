B.8 Which covector fields are differentials? (§5.2)

Appendix B • Proofs & Comments

B.8 Which covector fields are differentials? (§5.2)

In Section 4.2 we saw that, at a single point, every covector is a differential: for every \(\mathsf{X}_p \in T^*_p\mathcal{M}\) there is some \(f \in C^\infty(\mathcal{M})\) with \(\mathsf{X}_p = (df)_p\). It is natural to ask whether this statement survives the promotion from a point to the whole manifold:

Given a smooth covector field \(\mathsf{X} \in \Gamma T^*\mathcal{M}\), is there a single scalar field \(f \in C^\infty(\mathcal{M})\) such that \(\mathsf{X} = df\) everywhere?

The answer is “not always”, and it depends on the scale at which the question is asked: at a single point, on a small region around each point, or on the whole manifold. We go through the three scales in turn, and see that each one brings in a new obstruction. Technical names are introduced only at the end, once the ideas behind them are clear.

Two rules for computing differentials

Since \((df)_p(v_{\gamma, p}) \coloneqq v_{\gamma, p}(f) = (f \circ \gamma)'(\lambda_0)\), differentials inherit the rules of ordinary derivatives. For all scalar fields \(f, g\) and all \(\alpha \in \mathbb{R}\): \begin{align*} \underbrace{d\big( f \oplus_{C^\infty} \alpha \odot g \big) = df \oplus^*_\Gamma \alpha \odot^*_\Gamma dg}_{\text{linearity}}, \qquad\qquad \underbrace{d\big( f \odot_{C^\infty} g \big) = f \odot^*_\Gamma dg \oplus^*_\Gamma g \odot^*_\Gamma df}_{\text{Leibniz rule}}. \end{align*} The first follows from the linearity of the ordinary derivative, and the second, at each point \(p = \gamma(\lambda_0)\), from the product rule, \(\big( (f g) \circ \gamma \big)'(\lambda_0) = f(p) \cdot (g \circ \gamma)'(\lambda_0) + g(p) \cdot (f \circ \gamma)'(\lambda_0)\). In particular, the differential of a constant function vanishes, so constants can be moved freely in and out of \(d\). Both rules hold equally for functions defined only on an open set \(\mathcal{U}\), such as the coordinate maps \(\mathscr{X}^i\) of a chart. These two rules are all we need to understand what goes on below.

Scale 1: at a point, every covector is a differential

Fix a chart \((\mathcal{U}, \mathscr{X})\) around \(p\). The dual chart-induced basis consists of differentials already, those of the coordinate maps, and any covector at \(p\) is a combination of them, \(\mathsf{X}_p = \sum_i \mathtt{x}_i^p \cdot (d\mathscr{X}^i)_p\). The coefficients \(\mathtt{x}_i^p\) are real numbers, i.e. constants, so by linearity they can be moved inside the differential: \begin{align*} \mathsf{X}_p = \sum_{i=1}^d \mathtt{x}_i^p \cdot (d\mathscr{X}^i)_p = \big( d f_p \big)_p, \qquad \text{with} \qquad f_p \coloneqq \sum_{i=1}^d \mathtt{x}_i^p \odot \mathscr{X}^i. \end{align*} In the chart, \(f_p\) is simply the linear function whose slopes are the components of \(\mathsf{X}_p\), \((f_p \circ \mathscr{X}^{-1})(\alpha) = \sum_i \mathtt{x}_i^p \alpha^i\). It is defined only on \(\mathcal{U}\), but a bump function extends it to all of \(\mathcal{M}\) without changing its differential at \(p\) (see the footnote in Section 4.2). This is why it is legitimate to think of any covector at a point as a differential.

Scale 2: on a region, the coefficients stop being constants

Now consider a covector field \(\mathsf{X}\). Over a chart domain it decomposes as (Section 5.3) \begin{align*} \left. \mathsf{X} \right|_\mathcal{U} = \sum_{i=1}^d \mathtt{x}_i \odot^*_\Gamma d\mathscr{X}^i, \qquad \mathtt{x}_i \coloneqq \left. \mathsf{X} \right|_\mathcal{U} \left\lceil \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathscr{X}^i} \right\rfloor \in C^\infty(\mathcal{U}). \end{align*} At every point \(p \in \mathcal{U}\), the recipe of Scale 1 produces a function \(f_p\) with \((df_p)_p = \mathsf{X}_p\), but a different one for each point, since the slopes \(\mathtt{x}_i(p)\) change from point to point. The natural attempt to glue all of them into a single function is to replace the constant slopes by the component functions, \(f \coloneqq \sum_i \mathtt{x}_i \odot_{C^\infty} \mathscr{X}^i\). But now the coefficients are no longer constants, and the Leibniz rule produces an extra term: \begin{align*} d\Big( \sum_{i=1}^d \mathtt{x}_i \odot_{C^\infty} \mathscr{X}^i \Big) = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^d \mathtt{x}_i \odot^*_\Gamma d\mathscr{X}^i}_{\left. \mathsf{X} \right|_\mathcal{U}} \;\oplus^*_\Gamma\; \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^d \mathscr{X}^i \odot^*_\Gamma d\mathtt{x}_i}_{\text{extra term}}. \end{align*} The extra term comes entirely from the variation of the components across the region. This particular attempt fails, but some cleverer function might still work. To decide whether one does, we need a criterion.

A necessary condition. If \(\left. \mathsf{X} \right|_\mathcal{U} = df\) for some \(f\), then its components are not arbitrary: using \(df\lceil \mathcal{X} \rfloor = \mathcal{X}\lfloor f \rceil\), \begin{align*} \mathtt{x}_i = \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathscr{X}^i} \left\lfloor f \right\rceil, \qquad \text{i.e.} \qquad \mathtt{x}_i(p) = \partial_i \left[ f \circ \mathscr{X}^{-1} \right] \big( \mathscr{X}(p) \big), \end{align*} so all of them are partial derivatives of one and the same function \(f \circ \mathscr{X}^{-1}\). Differentiating once more, and using that mixed partial derivatives commute (Schwarz’s theorem), we obtain \begin{align*} \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathscr{X}^j} \left\lfloor \mathtt{x}_i \right\rceil = \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathscr{X}^i} \left\lfloor \mathtt{x}_j \right\rceil \qquad \forall \, i, j = 1, \dots, d. \tag{S} \end{align*} This symmetry condition does not depend on the chart used to write it, so it is a property of \(\mathsf{X}\) itself: under a change of chart, the Leibniz rule produces extra terms containing second derivatives of the chart transition map, which are symmetric in \(i\) and \(j\) and therefore cancel in the difference of both sides of (S).

Example. On \(\mathcal{M} = \mathbb{R}^2\) with the cartesian chart \(\mathscr{X}(p) = (a, b)\), consider the covector field with component functions \((\mathtt{x}_1, \mathtt{x}_2) = (-b, a)\), i.e. \(\mathsf{X} = (-\mathscr{X}^2) \odot^*_\Gamma d\mathscr{X}^1 \oplus^*_\Gamma \mathscr{X}^1 \odot^*_\Gamma d\mathscr{X}^2\). Then \begin{align*} \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathscr{X}^1} \left\lfloor \mathtt{x}_2 \right\rceil = 1 \;\neq\; -1 = \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathscr{X}^2} \left\lfloor \mathtt{x}_1 \right\rceil, \end{align*} so there is no scalar field \(f\) with \(\mathsf{X} = df\), neither on \(\mathbb{R}^2\) nor on any open set, however small. Pointwise, of course, nothing is wrong: at a point \(p\) with \(\mathscr{X}(p) = (a_p, b_p)\), Scale 1 gives \(\mathsf{X}_p = (df_p)_p\) with \(f_p = (-b_p) \odot \mathscr{X}^1 \oplus a_p \odot \mathscr{X}^2\). What fails is that these linear functions differ from point to point, and no single function reproduces all of them at once.

Exact fields do not form a module. The Leibniz rule also explains why the module \(\Gamma T^*\mathcal{M}\) of Section 5.2 had to be built from all smooth sections, and not only from differentials. Differentials are closed under addition, \(df \oplus^*_\Gamma dg = d(f \oplus_{C^\infty} g)\), but not under s-multiplication by scalar fields: rearranging the Leibniz rule, \begin{align*} h \odot^*_\Gamma df = d\big( h \odot_{C^\infty} f \big) \oplus^*_\Gamma (-f) \odot^*_\Gamma dh, \end{align*} which, because of the last term, is in general not a differential. For instance, \(\mathscr{X}^1 \odot^*_\Gamma d\mathscr{X}^2\) on \(\mathbb{R}^2\) has components \((\mathtt{x}_1, \mathtt{x}_2) = (0, a)\) and violates (S).

The condition is also sufficient on a ball. Conversely, suppose that \(\mathsf{X}\) satisfies (S) on a chart domain whose image \(\mathscr{X}(\mathcal{U})\) is a ball centred at the origin of \(\mathbb{R}^d\). Writing \(\bar{\mathtt{x}}_i \coloneqq \mathtt{x}_i \circ \mathscr{X}^{-1}\) for the chart representatives of the components, we can build the missing function explicitly, by adding up the values of \(\mathsf{X}\) along the straight segment from the origin to each point \(\alpha \in \mathscr{X}(\mathcal{U})\): \begin{align*} F(\alpha) \coloneqq \int_0^1 \sum_{i=1}^d \bar{\mathtt{x}}_i(t \alpha) \, \alpha^i \, dt, \qquad f \coloneqq F \circ \mathscr{X}. \end{align*} Differentiating under the integral sign, using (S) to exchange the indices, and recognizing a total derivative in \(t\), \begin{align*} \partial_j F(\alpha) &= \int_0^1 \Big[ \bar{\mathtt{x}}_j(t\alpha) + \sum_{i=1}^d t \, \alpha^i \, \partial_j \bar{\mathtt{x}}_i(t\alpha) \Big] dt \overset{\text{(S)}}{=} \int_0^1 \Big[ \bar{\mathtt{x}}_j(t\alpha) + \sum_{i=1}^d t \, \alpha^i \, \partial_i \bar{\mathtt{x}}_j(t\alpha) \Big] dt \\ &= \int_0^1 \frac{d}{dt} \Big[ t \, \bar{\mathtt{x}}_j(t\alpha) \Big] dt = \bar{\mathtt{x}}_j(\alpha), \end{align*} i.e. \(\mathtt{x}_j = \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathscr{X}^j} \lfloor f \rceil\), and hence \(\left. \mathsf{X} \right|_\mathcal{U} = df\). Since every point has a chart neighbourhood whose image is a ball, we conclude that at this scale (S) is both necessary and sufficient: a covector field is a differential near every point if and only if it satisfies (S).

Integrating a covector field along a curve

To understand the last scale we need one more tool, which in fact we have just used: the integral \(F(\alpha)\) above adds up the values of \(\mathsf{X}\) along a segment. In general, given a smooth curve \(\gamma\) and a parameter interval \([\lambda_a, \lambda_b]\), at each \(\lambda\) the covector \(\mathsf{X}_{\gamma(\lambda)}\) can eat the velocity of the curve, producing a real number. Adding up these numbers along the curve defines the integral of \(\mathsf{X}\) along \(\gamma\), \begin{align*} \int_\gamma \mathsf{X} \coloneqq \int_{\lambda_a}^{\lambda_b} \mathsf{X}_{\gamma(\lambda)}\big( v_{\gamma(\lambda)} \big) \, d\lambda = \int_{\lambda_a}^{\lambda_b} \sum_{i=1}^d \mathtt{x}_i\big(\gamma(\lambda)\big) \cdot \dot{\gamma}^i_\mathcal{U}(\lambda) \, d\lambda, \end{align*} where the second expression holds whenever the curve stays inside the chart domain \(\mathcal{U}\). If \(\mathsf{X} = df\), the integrand is, by definition of the differential and of the velocity, \((df)_{\gamma(\lambda)}\big( v_{\gamma(\lambda)} \big) = v_{\gamma(\lambda)}(f) = (f \circ \gamma)'(\lambda)\), and the fundamental theorem of calculus gives \begin{align*} \int_\gamma df = f\big(\gamma(\lambda_b)\big) - f\big(\gamma(\lambda_a)\big). \end{align*} The integral of a differential only depends on the endpoints of the curve, not on the path in between. In particular, for a closed curve, one that returns to its starting point, the integral of a differential always vanishes.

This also works in the opposite direction, and tells us how to build \(f\) from \(\mathsf{X}\) on a connected manifold. Fix a reference point \(p_0\), and define \(f(p)\) as the integral of \(\mathsf{X}\) along any curve from \(p_0\) to \(p\)1. This is a sensible definition precisely when the result does not depend on the curve chosen; and since two curves from \(p_0\) to \(p\) form a closed curve when the second is run backwards, this happens precisely when the integral of \(\mathsf{X}\) along every closed curve vanishes. In that case, extending a curve from \(p_0\) to \(p\) by a short segment along the \(j\)-th coordinate direction of a chart shows that \(\partial_j \left[ f \circ \mathscr{X}^{-1} \right] = \bar{\mathtt{x}}_j\), i.e. \(df = \mathsf{X}\). Therefore, \begin{align*} \mathsf{X} = df \text{ for some } f \in C^\infty(\mathcal{M}) \quad \iff \quad \int_\gamma \mathsf{X} = 0 \text{ for every closed curve } \gamma. \end{align*}

Scale 3: on the whole manifold, local primitives must glue

The construction on a ball worked because every point could be reached from the centre along a straight segment lying inside the region, so every closed curve in it could be shrunk continuously to a point, and (S) guarantees that the integral does not change during the shrinking, so it must vanish. On a region with a “hole”, a closed curve winding around the hole cannot be shrunk, and its integral may be non-zero even when (S) holds everywhere. In that case \(\mathsf{X}\) is a differential near every point (Scale 2), but the local functions cannot be glued into a single one.

Example. On the punctured plane \(\mathcal{M} = \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}\), with the same two charts as in Appendix C.4 (the cartesian chart \((\mathcal{U}, \mathscr{X})\), \(\mathscr{X}(p) = (a, b)\), and the polar chart \((\mathcal{V}, \mathscr{Y})\), defined off the ray \(\{a \leq 0, \, b = 0\}\)), consider the covector field with component functions \begin{align*} \left( \mathtt{x}_1, \mathtt{x}_2 \right) = \left( \frac{-b}{a^2 + b^2}, \frac{a}{a^2 + b^2} \right). \end{align*}

  • It satisfies (S) everywhere: \begin{align*} \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathscr{X}^1} \left\lfloor \mathtt{x}_2 \right\rceil = \frac{b^2 - a^2}{(a^2 + b^2)^2} = \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathscr{X}^2} \left\lfloor \mathtt{x}_1 \right\rceil. \end{align*} By Scale 2, it is therefore a differential near every point.

  • It is a differential on \(\mathcal{V}\): its components coincide with those of \(d\mathscr{Y}^2\), the second row of the Jacobian \(\left( \textrm{\runic ᛞ}_{\mathcal{V}\mathcal{U}} \right)^2_{j} = \partial \mathscr{Y}^2 / \partial \mathscr{X}^j\) computed in Appendix C.4. Hence \(\left. \mathsf{X} \right|_\mathcal{V} = d\mathscr{Y}^2\): on \(\mathcal{V}\), \(\mathsf{X}\) is the differential of the polar angle \(\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(b, a)\).

  • It is not a differential on \(\mathcal{M}\): along the unit circle \(\delta(\lambda) = (\cos \lambda, \sin \lambda)\), \(\lambda \in [0, 2\pi]\), whose velocity has components \(\dot{\delta}^i_\mathcal{U}(\lambda) = (-\sin \lambda, \cos \lambda)\), \begin{align*} \int_\delta \mathsf{X} = \int_0^{2\pi} \Big( (-\sin \lambda) \cdot (-\sin \lambda) + \cos \lambda \cdot \cos \lambda \Big) \, d\lambda = 2\pi \neq 0, \end{align*} although \(\delta\) is a closed curve.

The would-be function \(f\) is the angle \(\theta\) itself, and it fails for a simple reason. Near every point there is a local function whose differential is \(\mathsf{X}\), namely a local determination of the angle. Following the circle, each local determination must continue the previous one, and after a full turn we come back to the starting point with \(\theta + 2\pi\) instead of \(\theta\): the local functions exist, but they cannot be glued into a single-valued, smooth function on the whole punctured plane. The polar chart avoided the problem by cutting the plane along a ray, but that cut is precisely what makes it a local description.

The names of things

With the ideas in place, here is the standard vocabulary, which the reader will meet in the literature.

  • In the language of differential forms, covector fields are called 1-forms.

  • A covector field of the form \(\mathsf{X} = df\) is called exact.

  • A covector field satisfying (S) is called closed. The condition is usually written as \(d\mathsf{X} = 0\), where \(d\) is the exterior derivative, an operation extending the differential \(f \mapsto df\) from scalar fields to 1-forms. We have seen that every exact field is closed.

  • The Poincaré lemma states that on a contractible region – one that can be continuously shrunk to a point, such as a ball – every closed covector field is exact. The construction of \(F\) in Scale 2 is its proof for a ball.

  • The first de Rham cohomology group \(H^1_{\text{dR}}(\mathcal{M})\) collects the closed covector fields, regarding two of them as equivalent when they differ by an exact one. It is trivial precisely when every closed covector field is exact, and it measures, in this sense, the “one-dimensional holes” of \(\mathcal{M}\): it is trivial for \(\mathbb{R}^d\) or any contractible manifold, and non-trivial for the punctured plane or the circle \(S^1\).

In summary, the answer to our question depends on the scale at which it is asked, and each scale adds one requirement to the previous ones:

  1. At a point: every covector is a differential, \(\mathsf{X}_p = (df_p)_p\), because its components are constants and can be moved inside \(d\).

  2. Locally: once the components become functions, the Leibniz rule gets in the way, and a covector field is a differential near every point if and only if it is closed. Closed or not, every covector field is still locally a combination of differentials with function coefficients, \(\left. \mathsf{X} \right|_\mathcal{U} = \sum_i \mathtt{x}_i \odot^*_\Gamma d\mathscr{X}^i\): what may fail is being a single differential.

  3. Globally: a closed covector field is a differential on all of \(\mathcal{M}\) if and only if its integrals along all closed curves vanish, i.e. if and only if its local primitives can be glued together. This is guaranteed when \(H^1_{\text{dR}}(\mathcal{M})\) is trivial, e.g. on any contractible manifold, but even there only for closed fields: the field of the first example, on \(\mathbb{R}^2\), is not a differential anywhere.

This is why, in the main text, generic covector fields are denoted by \(\mathsf{X}, \mathsf{Y}, \dots\), and the symbol \(df\) is reserved for exact ones.

1

Here curves are allowed to be piecewise smooth, since joining two curves end to end generally produces a corner; the integral is then computed piece by piece.
