2.3 Bases, Components, and Transformation Laws

Introducing a basis for a vector space allows us to express vectors, covectors, and tensors in terms of components. This leads to the explicit study of how vectors, covectors, and tensors transform under changes of basis, and how their components encode the abstract multilinear structures defined previously (row 5 of Figure 1.2). Check appendices C.1.3 & C.1.4 for concrete examples showing how to extract coordinate components, and how to work with different bases.

  • Given a vector space \((V, \oplus, \odot)\), a subset \( B \subseteq V \) is a (Hamel-)5 basis if \begin{align*} \forall \mathcal{X} \in V, \, \exists ! \text{ finite } \{ \hat{x}_1, ..., \hat{x}_d \} \subset B : \exists ! \{ x^1, ..., x^d\} \in \mathbb{R} : \mathcal{X} = (x^1 \cdot \hat{x}_1) \oplus ... \oplus (x^d \cdot \hat{x}_d). \end{align*} If such a basis \(B\) exists, we call \(d\) the dimension of the vector space,and we say that \(\{ x^1, ..., x^d\}\) are the components of \(v\) w.r.t \(B\).

    Hence, we can decompose any vector \(\mathcal{X} \in V\) as \[ \mathcal{X} = \sum_{i=1}^d x^i \cdot \hat{x}_i \] where \(x^i \in \mathbb{R}\) are the components of \(\mathcal{X}\) w.r.t \(B\).

  • Let \(\{\hat{x}_1, \dots, \hat{x}_d\}\) and \(\{\hat{y}_1, \dots, \hat{y}_d\}\) be two bases of a vector space \((V, \oplus, \odot)\). Since both are bases, each vector of one basis can be uniquely expressed as a linear combination of the other. Therefore, there exists a matrix of real numbers \(J^j_{i}\) such that: \begin{align*} \hat{x}_i = \sum_{j=1}^d J^j_{i} \cdot \hat{y}_j , \quad \forall i = 1, \dots, d. \end{align*} We say that covariant objects follow this transformation law, and the matrix \(J\) defines the change of basis between \(\{\hat{x}_i\}\) and \(\{\hat{y}_i\}\).

    The matrix \(J^j_{i} \) is invertible– meaning that there exists a matrix \((J^{-1})^j_{i}\) such that6 \(J^i_{j} \cdot (J^{-1})^j_{i} = \delta^j_i\) – which is a direct consequence of the fact that both sets \(\{\hat{x}_i\}\) and \(\{\hat{y}_i\}\) are linearly independent and span the same vector space \(V\). Therefore, the inverse transformation law reads: \begin{align*} \hat{y}_i = \sum_{j=1}^d (J^{-1})^j_{i} \cdot \hat{x}_j, \quad \forall i = 1, \dots, d. \end{align*}

  • Let \(\mathcal{X} \in V\) be a vector with components \(\{x^i\}\) relative to the basis \(\{\hat{x}_i\}\) and components \(\{y^i\}\) relative to the basis \(\{\hat{y}_i\}\). The decomposition of \(\mathcal{X}\) w.r.t. both bases reads: \[ \mathcal{X} = \sum_{i=1}^d x^i \cdot \hat{x}_i = \sum_{i=1}^d y^i \cdot \hat{y}_i. \]

    Plugging the change of basis relation this expression gives: \[ \mathcal{X} = \sum_{i=1}^d x^i \cdot \sum_{j=1}^d J^j_{i} \cdot \hat{y}_j = \sum_{i=1}^d y^i \cdot \hat{y}_i. \] Since the basis vectors are linearly independent, we can equate the components on both sides to obtain the transformation law for the vector components: \[ y^i = \sum_{j=1}^d J^i_{j} \cdot x^j \longrightarrow x^i = \sum_{j=1}^d (J^{-1})^i_{j} \cdot y^j. \] We say that contravariant objects follow this transformation law.

  • Given a basis \(B\coloneq \{ \hat{x}_1, ..., \hat{x}_d \}\) of a vector space \((V, \oplus, \odot)\), we can define (it’s a convenient choice) the induced dual cobasis \(B^* \coloneq \{\hat{\mathfrak{x}}^1, ..., \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^d\}\) of its dual vector space \((V^*, \oplus^*, \odot^*)\) as : \[ \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^i (\hat{x}_j) = \delta^i_j = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } i = j, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

    Hence, we can decompose any covector \(\mathsf{X} \in V^*\) as \[ \mathsf{X} = \sum_{i=1}^d \mathtt{x}_i \cdot \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^i \] where \(\mathtt{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}\) are the components of \(\mathsf{X}\) w.r.t \(B^*\).

  • Let \(B_1^* \coloneq \{\hat{\mathfrak{x}}^1, \dots, \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^d\}\) and \(B_2^* \coloneq \{\hat{\mathfrak{y}}^1, \dots, \hat{\mathfrak{y}}^d\}\) be the dual bases associated to the bases \(B_1 \coloneq \{\hat{x}_1, \dots, \hat{x}_d\}\) and \(B_2 \coloneq \{\hat{y}_1, \dots, \hat{y}_d\}\), respectively. Since both are dual bases, each covector of one basis can be uniquely expressed as a linear combination of the other. Therefore, there exists a matrix of real numbers \(J^i_{j}\) (the same as for the vectors7) such that: \begin{align*} \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^i = \sum_{j=1}^d (J^{-1})^i_{j} \cdot \hat{\mathfrak{y}}^j \longrightarrow \hat{\mathfrak{y}}^i = \sum_{j=1}^d J^i_{j} \cdot \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^j, \quad \forall i = 1, \dots, d. \end{align*} As we previously defined it, we can observe that this transformation corresponds to that of a contravariant object.

  • Let \(\mathsf{X} \in V^*\) be a covector with components \(\{\mathtt{x}^i\}\) relative to the basis \(\{\hat{\mathfrak{x}}^i\}\), and components \(\{\mathtt{y}^i\}\) relative to the basis \(\{\hat{\mathfrak{y}}^i\}\). The decomposition of \(\mathsf{X}\) w.r.t. both bases reads: \[ \mathsf{X} = \sum_{i=1}^d \mathtt{x}_i \cdot \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^i = \sum_{i=1}^d \mathtt{y}_i \cdot \hat{\mathfrak{y}}^i. \] Plugging the change of basis relation into this expression gives: \[ \mathsf{X} = \sum_{i=1}^d \mathtt{x}_i \cdot \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^i = \sum_{i=1}^d \mathtt{y}_i \cdot \sum_{j=1}^d J^i_{j} \cdot \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^j. \] Since the basis vectors are linearly independent, we can equate the components on both sides to obtain the transformation law for the covector components: \[ \mathtt{x}_j = \sum_{i=1}^d J^i_{j} \cdot \mathtt{y}_i \longrightarrow \mathtt{y}_j = \sum_{i=1}^d (J^{-1})^i_{j} \cdot \mathtt{x}_i. \] Hence, we can observe that covector components transform as covariant objects.

  • Let \(\left\{ \hat{x}_1, \dots, \hat{x}_d \right\}\) be a basis of the vector space \((V, \oplus, \odot)\), and \(\left\{ \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^1, \dots, \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^d \right\}\) its associated dual basis in \((V^*, \oplus^*, \odot^*)\). Then the \(d^{\,r+s}\) tensors \[ \left\{ \hat{x}_{i_1} \otimes \dots \otimes \hat{x}_{i_r} \otimes \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^{j_1} \otimes \dots \otimes \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^{j_s} \;\Big|\; i_1,\dots,i_r, j_1,\dots,j_s \in \{1,\dots,d\} \right\} \] constitute a basis of \(T^{(r,s)}\). By the slot-by-slot action of simple tensors defined above, and \(\hat{\mathfrak{x}}^{k}(\hat{x}_{i}) = \delta^{k}_{i}\), each of them evaluates on basis elements as \begin{equation*} \left( \hat{x}_{i_1} \otimes \dots \otimes \hat{x}_{i_r} \otimes \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^{j_1} \otimes \dots \otimes \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^{j_s} \right) \left( \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^{k_1}, \dots, \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^{k_r}; \hat{x}_{l_1}, \dots, \hat{x}_{l_s} \right) = \delta^{k_1}_{i_1} \cdots \delta^{k_r}_{i_r} \cdot \delta^{j_1}_{l_1} \cdots \delta^{j_s}_{l_s}. \tag{$\star$} \end{equation*} Proof:

    • Spanning: Given \(T \in T^{(r,s)}\), define its components by evaluation on the basis, \[ T^{i_1 \dots i_r}_{j_1 \dots j_s} \coloneqq T \left( \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^{i_1}, \dots, \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^{i_r}; \hat{x}_{j_1}, \dots, \hat{x}_{j_s} \right) \in \mathbb{R}, \] and consider \[ S \coloneqq \sum_{\substack{i_1,\dots,i_r \\ j_1,\dots,j_s}} T^{i_1\dots i_r}_{j_1\dots j_s} \cdot \hat{x}_{i_1} \otimes \dots \otimes \hat{x}_{i_r} \otimes \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^{j_1} \otimes \dots \otimes \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^{j_s}. \] By \((\star)\), \(S\left( \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^{k_1}, \dots, \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^{k_r}; \hat{x}_{l_1}, \dots, \hat{x}_{l_s} \right) = T^{k_1\dots k_r}_{l_1\dots l_s} = T\left( \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^{k_1}, \dots, \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^{k_r}; \hat{x}_{l_1}, \dots, \hat{x}_{l_s} \right)\) for every choice of indices. Two multilinear maps that agree on all tuples of basis elements agree everywhere (expand each argument in its basis and use multilinearity slot by slot), hence \(T = S\).

    • Linear independence: Suppose a linear combination of these tensors vanishes: \[ \sum_{\substack{i_1,\dots,i_r \\ j_1,\dots,j_s}} \alpha^{i_1\dots i_r}_{j_1\dots j_s} \cdot \hat{x}_{i_1} \otimes \dots \otimes \hat{x}_{i_r} \otimes \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^{j_1} \otimes \dots \otimes \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^{j_s} = 0. \] Evaluating both sides on \(\left( \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^{k_1}, \dots, \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^{k_r}; \hat{x}_{l_1}, \dots, \hat{x}_{l_s} \right)\) and using \((\star)\), every term vanishes except the one with \(i_1 = k_1, \dots, j_s = l_s\), which yields \(\alpha^{k_1\dots k_r}_{l_1\dots l_s} = 0\) for all index combinations.

    Hence every \(T \in T^{(r,s)}\) decomposes uniquely as \[ T = \sum_{\substack{i_1,\dots,i_r \\ j_1,\dots,j_s}} T^{i_1\dots i_r}_{j_1\dots j_s} \cdot \hat{x}_{i_1} \otimes \dots \otimes \hat{x}_{i_r} \otimes \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^{j_1} \otimes \dots \otimes \hat{\mathfrak{x}}^{j_s}, \qquad \dim T^{(r,s)} = d^{\,r+s}. \]

  • A tensor \(T\) acts on any collection of \(r\) covectors \(\{\mathsf{X}^{(i)}\}\) and \(s\) vectors \(\{\mathcal{X}^{(j)}\}\) as: \[ \begin{aligned} T \left( \mathsf{X}^{(1)}, \dots, \mathsf{X}^{(r)}, \mathcal{X}^{(1)}, \dots, \mathcal{X}^{(s)} \right) = \sum_{\substack{i_1,\dots,i_r \\ j_1,\dots,j_s}} T^{i_1 \dots i_r}_{j_1 \dots j_s} \cdot \mathtt{x}_{i_1} \dots \mathtt{x}_{i_r} \cdot x^{j_1} \dots x^{j_s}. \end{aligned} \]

  • The components of a tensor \(T \in T^{(r,s)}\) transform according to: \begin{align*} {T}^{i_1 \dots i_r}_{j_1 \dots j_s} &= \sum_{\substack{k_1,\dots,k_r \\ l_1,\dots,l_s}} \left[ (J^{-1})^{i_1}_{k_1} \dots (J^{-1})^{i_r}_{k_r} \cdot J^{l_1}_{j_1} \dots J^{l_s}_{j_s} \cdot \widetilde{{T}}^{k_1 \dots k_r}_{l_1 \dots l_s} \right],\\ \widetilde{T}^{i_1 \dots i_r}_{j_1 \dots j_s} &= \sum_{\substack{k_1,\dots,k_r \\ l_1,\dots,l_s}} \left[ J^{i_1}_{k_1} \dots J^{i_r}_{k_r} \cdot (J^{-1})^{l_1}_{j_1} \dots (J^{-1})^{l_s}_{j_s} \cdot {T}^{k_1 \dots k_r}_{l_1 \dots l_s} \right], \end{align*} where \({T}\) are the components in the \(\{\hat{\mathfrak{x}}^i, \hat{x}_j\}\) basis and \(\widetilde{{T}}\) are the components in the \(\{\hat{\mathfrak{y}}^i, \hat{y}_j\}\) basis.

    This generalizes the transformation rules previously seen for vectors (contravariant) and covectors (covariant).
